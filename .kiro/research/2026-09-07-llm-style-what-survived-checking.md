## Tier A — asked of the primary, absent from our reports

### The feature-by-feature comparison

Zanotto and Aroyehun, *Linguistic and Embedding-Based Profiling of Texts Generated by Humans and Large
Language Models*, ACL. 467 985 texts, 14 971 human base documents, 8 domains, 11 models.

| Feature | Human | ChatGPT greedy | ChatGPT sampling | GPT-4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Text length (tokens) | 300.61 ± 2.766 | 260.35 ± 0.950 | 272.73 ± 0.960 | 289.58 ± 0.816 |
| Sentence length (tokens) | 23.53 ± 0.134 | 19.29 ± 0.065 | 20.00 ± 0.077 | 19.14 ± 0.038 |
| Morphological complexity, verbs | 8.73 ± 0.011 | 8.60 ± 0.009 | 8.70 ± 0.008 | 8.69 ± 0.008 |
| Morphological complexity, nouns | 8.73 ± 0.006 | 8.71 ± 0.006 | 8.80 ± 0.006 | 8.94 ± 0.004 |
| Word prevalence | 2.27 ± 0.001 | 2.28 ± 0.001 | 2.28 ± 0.001 | 2.27 ± 0.001 |
| Type-token ratio | 0.64 ± 0.001 | 0.62 ± 0.001 | 0.63 ± 0.001 | 0.65 ± 0.001 |
| Dependency tree depth | 6.83 ± 0.017 | 6.70 ± 0.012 | 6.75 ± 0.011 | 6.56 ± 0.011 |
| Dependency length | 2.41 ± 0.006 | 2.27 ± 0.004 | 2.32 ± 0.004 | 2.44 ± 0.004 |
| Sentence-to-sentence semantic similarity | 0.30 ± 0.001 | 0.38 ± 0.001 | 0.37 ± 0.001 | 0.35 ± 0.001 |
| Emotionality | 0.38 ± 0.001 | 0.37 ± 0.001 | 0.38 ± 0.001 | 0.39 ± 0.001 |

**Read this table before believing anything else about machine style.** Most features are nearly identical.
The standouts are **sentence-to-sentence semantic similarity** — machine text is markedly more internally
uniform in meaning — and **sentence length**, where machine sentences are about four tokens shorter. Tree
depth barely differs, and humans are *deeper*. Type-token ratio goes in different directions for different
models, which is why the "AI has a smaller vocabulary" claim and its opposite are both defensible.

### Detector unreliability

Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu and Zou, *GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers*, 2023.
Seven commercial detectors, accessed 2023-03-15, on 91 human TOEFL essays and 88 US 8th-grade essays.

- Average false-positive rate on the TOEFL essays: **61.22 %**. On the native-speaker essays: **5.19 %**.
- Per detector on TOEFL: Originality.ai 76 %, Quil.org 75 %, Sapling 68 %, OpenAI 58 %, Crossplag 52 %,
  GPTZero 52 %, ZeroGPT 48 %.
- All seven agreed on 18 of 91 essays (19.78 %); at least one flagged 89 of 91 (97.80 %).
- Prompting the model to *"enhance the word choices to sound more like that of a native speaker"* cut the
  average false-positive rate from **61.22 % to 11.77 %**, a 49.45-point drop.
- A second-round self-edit prompt, *"Elevate the provided text by employing literary language"*, cut
  detection of generated college essays from **100 % to 13 %**, averaging 3 % across detectors, on 31 essays.
- The same trick on scientific abstracts: **68 % to 28 %**, on 145 abstracts.

Dugan et al., *RAID: A Shared Benchmark for Robust Evaluation of Machine-Generated Text Detectors*, ACL
2024. 6 million generations, 11 models, 8 domains.

- Out-of-domain or unfamiliar generator: true-positive rate falls to **0 %** at a fixed 1 % false-positive
  rate.
- Table 16, accuracy at FPR = 5 %, unattacked → attacked:

| Detector | Baseline | Paraphrase | Synonym swap | Homoglyph |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Originality | 85.0 | 96.7 (+11.7) | 96.5 (+11.5) | **9.3 (−75.7)** |
| Binoculars | 79.6 | 80.3 (+0.7) | 43.5 (−36.1) | 37.7 (−41.9) |
| RADAR | 70.9 | 67.3 (−3.6) | 67.5 (−3.4) | 59.3 (−11.6) |
| GPTZero | 66.5 | 64.0 (−2.5) | 61.0 (−5.5) | 66.2 (−0.3) |
| GLTR | 62.6 | 47.2 (−15.4) | 31.2 (−31.4) | 24.3 (−38.3) |
| RoBERTa-Large (GPT2) | 56.7 | 72.9 (+16.2) | 79.4 (+22.7) | 21.3 (−35.4) |

Accuracy **rising** under paraphrase and synonym attacks is not an Originality-only quirk — RoBERTa-Large
rose more. The likely reason is that the paraphraser leaves its own detectable trace, but no source in the
corpus says so, so that is an inference and not a finding.

- Originality's other failures: **8.5 %** accuracy on Python code, **13.0 %** false-positive rate on
  Wikipedia under a single global threshold, and a floor of **0.62 %** on how low its FPR can be set.
- German, 1 970 articles from the *DER STANDARD* corpus, accuracy at FPR = 5 %: Originality 89.1 %,
  Binoculars 76.7 %, FastDetectGPT 75.5 %, Winston 73.8 %, GLTR 68.9 %, RADAR 53.2 %, ZeroGPT 51.7 %,
  RoBERTa-B CGPT 51.5 %, GPTZero 49.5 %, RoBERTa-L GPT2 48.4 %, RoBERTa-B GPT2 39.7 %, LLMDet 10.6 %.

Sandhan et al., *Can We Trust LLM Detectors?*: adding quotation marks and attribution cues drops detection
accuracy to **8.5 %**; typographical noise drops it to **3.3 %**; a white-box gradient attack flips
**99.3 %** of classifications.

Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers and Goldstein, *A Watermark for Large Language Models*, 2023:
**98.63 %** classification accuracy at a **1.0 %** false-positive rate. Under a T5 span-replacement attack,
detection AUC falls from 0.998 unattacked to 0.954, 0.838 and 0.696 at replacement budgets of 0.3, 0.5 and
0.7.

Sadasivan, Kumar, Balasubramanian, Wang and Feizi, *Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?*, 2023:
an information-theoretic argument that the best possible detector approaches AUROC 0.50 as models improve.

### Lexical diversity, and why two papers disagree

Kendro, Maloney and Jarvis, *Do LLMs produce texts with "human-like" lexical diversity?*, 2025. 240 human
TOEFL essays against 120 ChatGPT outputs across four model generations.

| Measure | LLM | Human |
|---|---|---|
| Volume (tokens) | 465.53 ± 88.51 | 273.01 ± 33.26 |
| Abundance (types) | 249.32 ± 49.35 | 130.97 ± 20.03 |
| MATTR | 42.99 ± 1.75 | 38.49 ± 1.89 |
| Evenness (Jost's entropy) | 0.98 ± 0.01 | 0.97 ± 0.01 |
| Disparity | 1.04 ± 0.01 | 1.03 ± 0.01 |
| Dispersion (gap between repetitions) | 6.87 ± 1.98 | **16.39 ± 4.14** |

Machine text is *more* lexically diverse on five of six measures and drastically less **dispersed** — words
recur much closer together. The human comparison group is second-language writers, which matters.

Turós, Soltész-Várhelyi and Szűts, *Does the Thesis Still Make Sense?*, MDPI. 16 essays, 391 reviewers,
741 evaluations, **Hungarian**, models Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT o3-mini.

- Maas TTR: human 0.169, AI 0.111. MSTTR human 0.774. Hapax legomena human 0.297.
- Blind quality ratings, 1–5: AI scored **higher** on scholarly dimensions (4.22 vs 3.97, p < 0.001) and
  **lower** on readability (3.77 vs 3.94, p = 0.018) and originality (3.55 vs 3.80, p = 0.001).

**These two papers report opposite directions**, on different genres with different metrics — and the MDPI
paper cites Kendro for the claim that LLMs have *lower* diversity, which is the opposite of what Kendro
found. That miscitation is in the published literature, not in our pipeline.

### Register: what actually shifts

Milička, Marklová and Cvrček, *Benchmark of stylistic variation in LLM-generated texts*, 2025. 16 frontier
models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and DeepSeek, against the BE21 English and Koditex Czech
multi-genre corpora.

English, on Biber's **six** dimensions: shifts toward informational production (D1), **explicit reference
(D3, the strongest and most consistent)**, and non-abstract information (D5). D2 (narrative) and D6 (online
elaboration) barely move — those are easy to imitate. Czech, on Cvrček's **eight** dimensions: toward higher
cohesion, general/intension and prospective; away from polythematic, addressee coding and attitudinal.

- **Czech shifts much further than English.** Some configurations failed on all eight dimensions. The best
  Czech result, Claude 3 Opus at temperature 1, reached an average normalised shift near **40** — barely
  better than the *worst*-performing models in English.
- **Sampling temperature had no systematic effect.** Temperature 0 and 1 clustered together.
- **The system prompt moves style more than the model generation does.** GPT-3.5-turbo with the leaked
  ChatGPT system prompt is stylistically *less* similar to itself under a minimal prompt than to newer
  OpenAI models. The minimal prompt, verbatim: *"Please continue the text in the same manner and style,
  ensuring it contains at least five thousand words. The text does not need to be factually correct, but
  please make sure it fits stylistically."*
- **Why Czech is worse is conjecture, not measurement.** The paper attributes it to English dominance in
  pre-training (93 % in GPT-3) and to BPE tokenisation designed around English, both cited from other
  papers. What the authors measured is the shift itself. Asked directly whether anyone has measured the
  relationship between a language's training-data share and its stylistic deviation: **nobody has.**

The first row is not a model. It is the distance between two halves of the same human corpus — the amount

instruction-tuned configuration is four to five times further out.

The paper's own sentence: *"However, the best overall performance was achieved by LLaMA 3.1 Completion,
which is also a base model."* Section 4.2, *Base versus instruction tuned*. Sixteen frontier models, against
BE21 English (500 samples) and Koditex Czech (676 samples).

The distinction that decides whether the claim is about training or about model choice:

| Comparison | Base | Instruction-tuned | Same model? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Llama 3.1 405B, 16-bit | **7.1** (complete, t = 1.0) | **38.3** (t = 1.0), **38.9** (t = 0.0) | **Yes** |
| GPT-3.5-turbo | 20.9 (completion, t = 1.0) | 37.4 (chat, t = 1.0 and 0.0) | **No** — `gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct` against `gpt-3.5-turbo-16k-0613` |
| davinci-002 vs GPT-3.5-turbo | 17.3 (complete, t = 1.0) | 39.8 (default system prompt) | **No** — different models |

**One clean comparison exists, and it is enormous: 31.2 to 31.8 points on the same 405-billion-parameter
model.** The other two look like the same finding and are not; they compare different models that happen to
differ in alignment. Asked directly, the corpus said so for each — which is why the question has to name
the requirement rather than ask for "evidence".

### The qualification that makes "base models are more human-like" too simple

Zanotto and Aroyehun's Table 1, greedy decoding, average sentence length in tokens:

| Model | Sentence length |
|---|---|
| Human | 23.53 ± 0.134 |
| Mistral-7B-v0.1 (base) | **50.66 ± 0.718** |
| Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1 | 23.17 ± 0.176 |
| mpt-30b (base) | **43.87 ± 0.660** |
| mpt-30b-chat | 22.06 ± 0.113 |

Under greedy decoding, base models **loop and mode-collapse**, producing sentences twice human length; the
instruction-tuned variants sit almost exactly on the human figure. Both pairs are the same underlying model.

So the honest statement is narrower than the headline: **base models match human register better when they
generate successfully, and fail catastrophically when they do not.** Czech generation with davinci-002 at
temperature 0 collapsed into constant sentence repetition. Alignment trades register fidelity for
reliability, and which one matters depends on what the text is for.

### Translationese: the proportions themselves

Valentini, Wright, Granados, Colunga and von der Wense. Five languages — **English, German, Spanish, Greek
and Pashto** — with Gemini-2.0-Flash-001 and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct.

The metric is an SVM trained to separate translated from natively-written text; the reported quantity is
**Classified Positive** — the share of generations the classifier labels as translated. Native generation,
no translation step:

| Language | Native human | Human translation | Gemini | Llama | Test sample |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| English | 0 % | 99.4 % | 24.5 % | 27.7 % | 800 + 800 |
| **German** | 0 % | 99.9 % | **60.2 %** | **37.6 %** | 800 + 800 |
| **Spanish** | 0 % | 99.7 % | **56.6 %** | **34.7 %** | 800 + 800 |
| Greek | 0 % | 98.6 % | 25.4 % | 28.9 % | 700 + 700 |
| Pashto | 9.4 % | 87.5 % | 28 % | 29 % | 100 + 100 |

The classifier is calibrated: native human text scores 0 % in four languages, human translation 98–100 %.
So the middle column is a real measurement and not an artefact.

**German and Spanish are the outliers, and English, Greek and Pashto are not.** Gemini's German output is
flagged as translated 60 % of the time against 24.5 % for its English. But Greek at 25.4 % and Pashto at
28 % sit at English levels — so the effect is **not** a monotonic function of how low-resource a language is.
That is my reading of the numbers as given; the paper's stated mechanism is an internal English
representation, which would predict the low-resource languages to be worse, and they are not. **The
mechanism is not settled by this table.**

One anomaly worth not explaining away: Llama's Spanish output *after* a machine-translation step scores
**21.9 %**, lower than its native Spanish generation at 34.7 %, while the same pairing in German goes to
99.1 %. No source in the corpus accounts for that.

When an LLM is used explicitly as the translator of native human text (Europarl-UdS): Gemini German 76.0 %,
Spanish 89.7 %; Llama German 60.6 %, Spanish 87.8 %.

### Writing to avoid sounding like a model, measured

*MACHINE SPEECH, HUMAN SPEECH*, a master's thesis, full text in the corpus. **105** English-speaking
participants from Prolific, aged 18–81 (mean 39.3, SD 14.3), in a chain-generation design borrowed from the
iterated-learning paradigm: each participant edits the previous one's text to make it read as
human-authored, and the chain is measured across generations.

| What was measured | Coefficient | Test | p | After Holm correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lexical diversity (MTLD) | **b = −10.40**, SE 1.33 | t(83) = −7.82 | **< .001** | **< .001** |
| Syntactic complexity (mean dependency distance) | b = −0.005, SE 0.012 | t(83) = −0.39 | .699 | 1.000 |
| Error rate (Poisson, log word offset) | b = 0.167, SE 0.064 | z = 2.62 | .009 | .061 |

**Trying to sound less like a language model measurably narrows your vocabulary and changes nothing else.**
The MTLD decline is large and survives correction. Syntax does not move at all. The error-rate increase
fails Holm correction — so the widely-circulated advice to insert deliberate typos has, in the one
experiment here that tests the strategy, no significant effect on the axis it targets and a significant cost
on another.

What people reported dropping, in the thesis's own words:

> "The same retreat was reported anecdotally by individuals on social media, who described deliberately
> dropping words like delve … or the em dash … due to their becoming commonly associated with LLMs."

So the folk signals are **"delve"** and the **em dash**. Note the direction of causation: these are humans
abandoning their own usage because a machine adopted it, which is a measurable change in human writing
caused by models — the same phenomenon Mak and Walasek found in student reports, running the other way.

### Detection failure is a property of the pairing, not of the detector

*Why AI-Generated Text Detection Fails: Evidence from Explainable AI Beyond Benchmark Accuracy*, **arXiv
preprint** (March 2026, revised April 2026), full text in the corpus. Four classical classifiers on a
30-feature linguistic set, 10 000 balanced test documents, trained on 23 707 PAN-CLEF and 610 767 COLING
samples. English only.

In-distribution it works well — SVM 96.55 % accuracy, 97.34 F1, 6.03 % false-positive rate, second on the
Voight–Kampff leaderboard against a Binoculars LLaMA 3.1 baseline of 86.60. On COLING, F1 drops to 80.25.

The finding is in the cross-generator false-negative rates:

| Generator | Best-PAN configuration | Best-COLING configuration |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-5.2 | **23.25 %** missed | 3.42 % missed |
| DeepSeek V3.2 | 0.76 % | 3.51 % |
| Gemini 3 Pro | 0.43 % | **18.40 %** |

**The two configurations fail on opposite generators.** The one that catches Gemini 3 Pro almost perfectly
misses nearly a quarter of GPT-5.2, and the one that catches GPT-5.2 misses nearly a fifth of Gemini. There
is no ordering of detectors by quality here — only detector-generator pairs, and a deployment cannot know in
advance which generator it is facing.

### Argument structure separates more sharply than style

*AI Argues Differently: Distinct Argumentative and Linguistic Patterns of LLMs in Persuasive Contexts*, ACL
Anthology, full text in the corpus. 13 498 human original posts, 26 996 human counter-arguments, 40 494
parallel LLM counter-arguments from GPT-3.5-turbo, LLAMA2-7B and Mistral-7B-instruct. 301 general
linguistic features plus 27 argument-quality features. Separation reported as Wasserstein-1 distance.

Largest gaps, machine **lower** than human unless marked: impact 0.31, interactivity 0.28, effectiveness
0.24, explicit respect 0.23, quality 0.20, overall 0.20. Machine **higher**: referencing discourse
participants 0.35 — the single largest gap — implicit respect 0.21, type-token ratio 0.20, lemma-token ratio
0.19, POS variability 0.12, Herdan's C 0.11.

Classifier accuracy: argument-quality features alone **98.08 %**, linguistic features alone **98.85 %**,
both together **99.27 %**. Out of domain: Yelp 75.7 F1, ELI5 83.1, WritingPrompts 93.5, CMV 87.8, average
81.1 — the same generalisation collapse everything else in this corpus reports.

**Machine argument references its interlocutor more and persuades less.** And the third independent source
now measures type-token ratio as *higher* in machine text.

### What the Czech readers actually got wrong

Milička, Marklová, Drobil and Pospíšilová. 254 Czech native speakers, randomised into immediate-feedback
and no-feedback cohorts. Correctness: **no feedback 55.4 % (95 % CI 52.0–58.7)**, **feedback 65.1 %
(95 % CI 62.4–67.8)**.

Their misconceptions, in the paper's words:

> "Participants assign AI generated texts greater readability but at the same time suppose that the more
> readable texts are written by human."

> "…without feedback, participants may rely on prior assumptions about AI writing — namely, that AI tends to
> produce static texts characterized by a high density of nominal or phrasal constructions."

> "This finding supports the idea that the no feedback group associate prepared, formal styles — such as
> those typical of scientific or administrative texts — with AI."

And the calibration failure, precisely located:

> "The greatest difference between the groups appears at confidence level seven, where no feedback group
> participants were overconfident and made the most errors precisely when they were most certain (even
> below the chance level of 0.5)…"

**Below chance at maximum confidence.** The cue people reach for — formal, nominal, prepared-sounding prose
reads as machine-written — is not merely weak, it is inverted, and the same readers rate the machine text as
*more* readable while treating readability as evidence of a human author.

## Tier B — stated in a primary source, no table demanded

Mak and Walasek, 2025. 4 820 UK undergraduate psychology reports, 2021–2025.

- Nominalisations, syntactic complexity and ChatGPT-associated lexis rose sharply in 2023–24, easing in
  2025.
- **Grades and tutor feedback stayed flat.** Writing more like a model changed nothing about outcomes.

Human discrimination accuracy, across sources: untrained readers **50.21 %** on poetry and **51–52 %** on
short excerpts; trained applied linguists **38.9 %** on abstracts, i.e. below chance; peer reviewers **68 %**
on abstracts rising to **86 %** on full articles; and five experienced LLM users identified **299 of 300**
articles correctly by majority vote.

**So "does it read as human" has no general answer.** It depends almost entirely on who is reading, and
people who use language models daily are close to infallible at spotting their output.

## Tier C — rests only on a compiled report

Reinhart, Markey, Laudenbach, Pantusen, Yurko, Weinberg and Brown, *Do LLMs write like humans? Variation in
grammatical and rhetorical styles*, PNAS 2025.

**The paper is in the notebook as an abstract and metadata page only — its full text is absent.** Asked for
its own tables, the corpus answered *"not covered"*. Every figure below therefore comes from our own
compiled report or from another paper citing it, and none has been checked against the paper.

- Present participial clauses at **2–5×** the human rate; nominalisations at **1.5–2×**.
- A random forest on Biber features separates human from machine chunks at **66 %** accuracy against a
  14 % chance baseline. Parallel corpora HAP-E (n = 8 290) and CAP (n = 9 615), six genres, Llama 3 and
  GPT-4o variants.
- Patterns held as model size grew within a family.
- **Instruction tuning makes models easier to distinguish from humans than their untuned base
  counterparts.**
- A separate paper in the corpus repeats a related claim: GPT-4o and GPT-4o Mini *"overused words like
  'camaraderie' and 'palpable' at greater than 100 times human rates"*, and Llama base models emulated
  human text better than instruction-tuned variants. Also a citation of Reinhart, not an independent
  measurement.

The last bullet is the causal claim the whole picture rests on. **It is not yet sourced to the paper** — but
it no longer rests on this tier alone: the Llama 3.1 same-model comparison in tier A supports it
independently, and the mode-collapse table qualifies it.

**The cheap route to the paper's own tables is closed.** Reinhart's own page at refsmmat.com *is* in the
corpus in full text, but it holds only qualitative summaries: *"An analysis of LLM-generated writing in a
parallel corpus, using grammatical and rhetorical features (not just vocabulary, word length, etc.)."* No
feature tables. The only remaining route is adding a full-text URL for the paper by hand.

## Swedish: a documented absence

Asked directly, twice, with the answer required to be explicit:

> There is an absolute gap in the empirical, peer-reviewed literature regarding the measurement of
> stylistic and grammatical signatures in machine-generated Swedish text.

And, more sharply: the *Nordic Journal of Linguistics* states that multidimensional analysis has **never
been applied to Swedish data of any kind**. Norwegian, Danish and Dutch are equally empty. Finnish has a
register model (FinCORE, 10 754 web texts) and a three-factor model for constrained Finnish, neither used
for human-machine comparison. German is the only Germanic language with any measurement — via RAID, and via
the translationese proportions above.

**Confirmed a fourth time, from three sources never previously queried.** Asked whether the thesis on
human-versus-machine speech, the detection-failure preprint, or the argumentation paper report any Nordic or
Germanic language beyond English and German, the answer was `"not covered"` three times. The argumentation
paper states it in its own limitations: *"Due to data availability, the present work only considers English
arguments."* The detection preprint restricts itself to PAN-CLEF, COLING and Ghostbuster, all English.

**This gap should now be treated as established rather than re-searched.** Six independent angles — two
Deep Research runs targeted at Nordic and Germanic languages, and four primary sources asked directly — have
each returned nothing.

The nearest Swedish-adjacent material is a paraphrase pilot on 139 sentence pairs (Mixtral 7B, GPT-SW3
6.7B), the DaLAJ acceptability dataset, and a 2021 literary-translation study using 2017-era Google
Translate. None measures style.

**What would settle it**, per the benchmark's own method: a Biber-style dimension model over a multi-genre
Swedish reference corpus, a parallel corpus of model continuations, normalised shift vectors, and a test of
whether Swedish base models produce coherent text at all — which Czech base models largely did not.

## Tier D — checked and found wrong

**Dependency tree depth.** Our own report states, attributed by name to a real paper that is in the corpus
in full text:

> "Average syntactic complexity … is **deeper and more uniform in model-generated texts** … Mean depth of
> 6.1 ± 1.1 for ChatGPT versus 4.8 ± 1.1 for humans."

The paper's own Table 1 gives human **6.83 ± 0.017** and ChatGPT **6.70 ± 0.012**. The direction is
reversed, the values are wrong, and the error bars are about sixty-five times too wide. None of 6.83, 6.70,
6.56 or 0.017 appears anywhere in our reports, so this was not a mis-transcription of the paper.

It travelled four hops without contradiction: our report, then an AI-generated topic-summary page in the
corpus, then a chat answer quoting that page, then a session summary calling it established. Each hop
faithfully quoted the previous one.

**A wrong magnitude is visible; a wrong sign is not.** A reversed finding is exactly as coherent as the real
one and fits any argument built around it. This one was load-bearing for a conclusion about deep structure
being un-humanlike, and the real table shows most features nearly identical with the largest gap elsewhere.

### The second one: an index-shifted table, where every individual number is real

Our run-3 report reproduces the benchmark's English shift table like this:

| Report's label | Report's value | What that value actually belongs to |
|---|---|---|
| gemini-2.0-flash-exp 1.0 | 7.1 | `llama-3.1-405b complete 1.0` |
| davinci-002 (complete) 1.0 | 7.2 | *Original corpus: first vs. second part* — the human baseline |
| llama-3.1-405b 1.0 | 33.5 | `gemini-1.5-pro-002 1.0` |
| llama-3.1-405b 0.0 | 33.8 | `gpt-4-turbo 1.0` |

The value column is correct and in the source's order. The label column is shifted, because the report
**dropped the first row — the human-vs-human baseline — and did not shift the labels with it.** Every number
is genuine. Every pairing is wrong. And the report ends up presenting the human baseline as a model score,
and the worst instruction-tuned configuration as the best.

The verifiable trace: `38.3`, `37.4` and `39.8` appear in none of our reports, and neither does the string
`Original corpus` — so the corrected table came from the primary database, not from re-reading our own text.

**This is harder to catch than the sign reversal.** A reversed sign is at least *implausible* if you know the
field. A transposed table is internally consistent, arithmetically sound, and every figure survives a
spot-check against the source. Only the *pairing* is false, and pairing is exactly what a spot-check of
individual numbers does not test.

What caught it: quoting both conflicting claims back verbatim in the same question, naming the numbers, and
forbidding reconciliation. The answer then produced the source's own row order — which is the only view in
which a shift is visible.

**The rule this yields: when a table is the evidence, ask for its rows in the source's own order, including
any baseline or reference row.** A table quoted without its baseline row has lost the thing that makes its
numbers mean anything — here, that the best model sits inside the range a human corpus differs from itself.

### The third: a confident wrong title beside a wrong source kind

The same report's inventory lists the Czech feedback study as *"Learning to detect AI texts and learning the
limits"*, 2025, **"Peer-reviewed journal article"**. The work is an arXiv preprint titled *"Humans can learn
to detect AI-generated texts, or at least learn when they can't"*. Neither the real title nor anything close
to it appears in any of our reports.

This is the run in which the title clause was active, and it worked seven times — seven rows wrote
`"title not verified"`. This row instead asserted a fabricated title *and* upgraded a preprint to a
peer-reviewed article. **The clause reduces confident invention; it does not eliminate it.** Where the source
kind matters to how much weight a claim gets, verify it separately from the title.

## Still contradictory, deliberately unresolved

**Lexical diversity.** Kendro measures machine text as *more* diverse on five of six measures; the
argumentation paper also puts type-token ratio, lemma-token ratio and Herdan's C higher in machine text; the
MDPI paper reports lower diversity and cites Kendro for it. **Two of three now point the same way, against
the MDPI prose.** One caveat I cannot resolve from the corpus: MDPI's own measure is the **Maas** index,
whose scale may run opposite to the others, in which case its numbers might agree with Kendro while its
sentences do not. That is worth one question and is not settled here.

**Detector accuracy rising under attack.** Originality rose from 85.0 to 96.7 under paraphrase, and
RoBERTa-Large rose further. That the paraphraser leaves its own detectable trace is my inference; no source
in the corpus says it.

**The mechanism behind elevated German and Spanish translationese.** The paper proposes an internal English
representation; its own proportions show Greek and Pashto at English levels, which that mechanism does not
predict. Recorded as an open question, not resolved in either direction.

## Applying this: a prompt, and what each line rests on

**Derived, not a finding.** The block below is one application of the tiers above, written 2026-09-08. It has
been run **zero times** — nothing about the prompt itself has been measured, only the features it targets.
Two of its ten instructions rest on nothing in this corpus at all, and are marked so.

Written for prose quality, not for defeating a detector. That is not a hedge: detection collapses from a
single prompt line anyway (100 % to 13 %, tier A above), so evasion is not an optimisation problem, and the
instructions that make prose less machine-like are the same ones that make it worth reading.

```text
You are continuing a piece of writing in an established voice.

VOICE SAMPLE - match this, do not describe it:
[paste 300+ words of the target author's own prose]

TASK: [what to write]

Constraints, most important first.

1. Let consecutive sentences be about different things. Do not have each sentence
   restate, elaborate or gloss the one before it. When a point is made, move to the
   next one instead of re-approaching the last from another angle.
2. Do not spell out connections the reader can infer. Cut "as mentioned above",
   "the aforementioned", "this means that", "it is important to note that". Use a
   pronoun where the antecedent is obvious instead of repeating the noun. Never
   summarise a paragraph at its own end.
3. No present participial clause tacked onto a main clause. Not ", highlighting the
   need for X" or ", making it possible to Y". Write a second sentence instead.
4. Prefer verbs to nominalisations. "We decided", not "the decision was made".
   "Because it failed", not "due to the failure of".
5. Where both fit, choose the Germanic word over the Romance one: use not utilise,
   show not demonstrate, start not commence, need not necessitate, about not
   regarding, enough not sufficient.
6. Do not reuse a distinctive word close to its last appearance. If the concept
   returns within a few sentences, restructure rather than repeat the term.
7. Let sentences run slightly longer than your default. Do not compress.
8. Do not address the reader. No "you might wonder", no "it is worth considering",
   no "let us".
9. Never use: delve, tapestry, testament, underscore, pivotal, crucial, robust,
   nuanced, landscape, realm, leverage as a verb, navigate figuratively. No em
   dashes. No "not only ... but also". No three-item list where two items suffice.
10. Do not open by restating the task. Do not close with a summary or a
    forward-looking sentence.

Do not introduce errors, typos or forced informality.

Length: [N] words. Output the text only.
```

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sentence-to-sentence semantic similarity: human 0.30, machine 0.35–0.38, on 467 985 texts. **The largest gap in the whole table** | **A** |
| 2 | Explicit reference (D3) is the strongest and most consistent English register shift | **A** |
| 3 | Present participial clauses at 2–5× the human rate | **C** — the paper is metadata-only |
| 4 | Nominalisations at 1.5–2× the human rate | **C** — same source |
| 5 | Base-to-instruct shift toward Romance-origin vocabulary across model families | **C** — never asked of the primary |
| 6 | Dispersion: machine repeats a word after 6.87 words against 16.39 for humans | **A** |
| 7 | Sentence length: human 23.53 tokens, machine 19.14–20.00 | **A** |
| 8 | Underproduced conversational involvement; and machine argument references its interlocutor *more* than human argument does (0.35, the largest gap in that paper) | **A** |
| 9 | `delve` and the em dash are the two signals humans reported abandoning. The rest of the list is **my own observation, measured nowhere in this corpus** | A for two items, **unsourced** for the rest |
| 10 | **Unsourced.** My own observation | **unsourced** |

Instruction 7 deliberately gives no word count. The measured unit is **tokens**, which include punctuation,
so converting to words would invent a figure — the same unit error that cost a day on the site's page count.

### Deliberately excluded, with the reason

- **"Vary your sentence length" / burstiness.** The most repeated advice in circulation. Sentence-length
  *variance* parameters were **confirmed absent from this corpus twice**. Means exist; spreads were never
  published. Nothing supports it.
- **Deliberate typos and forced informality.** The one experiment that tests the strategy — 105 participants
  editing toward human-sounding prose — found the error-rate change **non-significant after Holm correction**,
  while lexical diversity fell sharply (b = −10.40, p < .001). No measured benefit, a measured cost.
- **"Use simpler words."** Wrong direction. Three sources now measure lexical diversity as *higher* in machine
  text. The problem is uniformity of meaning, not vocabulary size.

### The structural lever matters more than any line above

The most human-like configurations measured were **base models in completion mode**, and the system prompt
moves style further than the choice of model does — GPT-3.5 under a long system prompt clusters with newer
models rather than with itself under a minimal one. The benchmark's own minimal prompt was
*"Please continue the text in the same manner and style."*

So handing the model a sample of the target voice and asking it to continue is doing more work than the ten
constraints. Both tier A.

### Language

Every figure above is measured on **English**. For Swedish there is nothing, established from six angles.
What little is known about other languages points the wrong way: Czech register shifts roughly twice as far
as English, and German model output is flagged as translated 60.2 % of the time against 24.5 % for English.
Applied to Swedish this prompt is a reasonable guess, not a grounded recommendation.
