This corpus-based evaluation demonstrates that machine-generated news texts default to factual, event-driven reporting structures (answering the basic query components of "who, what, when, and where") rather than replicating the interpretive, highly personalized, and editorial narrative styles characteristic of campus feature writing [cite: 9]. This systematic mismatch suggests that the models mirror the dominant conventions of their massive event-driven journalistic pre-training corpora [cite: 9]. Consequently, register-level signals, such as the absence of student-oriented contextual references and stylistic framing, provide a highly robust diagnostic for identifying automated text, complementing and bypassing the volatility of surface-level statistical detection algorithms [cite: 9].Noun-Heavy Nominalization in Instruction-Tuned ModelsInstruction-tuned language models produce text characterized by a dense, noun-heavy, highly nominalized grammatical style, even when explicitly prompted to simulate highly informal conversational speech or narrative writing [cite: 16]. This structural pattern is shown to persist across model families, representing a fundamental "persona-alignment constraint" inherent to current preference optimization pipelines [cite: 16, 17]. The systematic overproduction of abstract nouns, prepositions, and attributive adjectives at the expense of verbs and pronouns demonstrates that the alignment process (such as reinforcement learning from human feedback) permanently biases the underlying language distributions toward informational density [cite: 17]. Structurally, the AI-authored misinformation is characterized by a high frequency of unsubstantiated doubts and sudden logical leaps, frequently jumping to unfounded conclusions without presenting intermediate empirical evidence [cite: 8].
